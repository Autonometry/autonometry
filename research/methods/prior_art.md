# Prior art: how robot autonomy and human intervention are defined and measured today

Autonomy Meter project, Part 1. Retrieval date for every source: 2026-10-30. Tags: **VERIFIED** means the primary text was read (regulation, standard excerpt, full paper); **CLAIM** means only the official scope page, abstract, or a secondary reproduction was read; **UNKNOWN** means the primary text could not be retrieved. Bibliographic details for every citation are in `sources.csv` (same ids as the bracketed labels below).

## Summary

No existing standard, regulation or paper measures the quantity Autonomy Meter targets: the share of a robot's work performed by its AI versus humans, with a confidence interval and a tamper-evident record. The closest antecedents are (i) NIST ALFUS, which names "ratio of human intervention time / mission time" as a metric but never specifies an estimator or interval [NIST-SP1011-II, VERIFIED]; (ii) California's disengagement reporting, which produced the only large public event-plus-exposure dataset, but which the DMV itself says is not designed for cross-company comparison and which California is now replacing with failure-, immobilization- and mileage-based monthly reports [DMV-NEWS-2026, DMV-REG-CURRENT, VERIFIED]; and (iii) the human-in-the-loop robot-learning literature, which logs interventions at timestep resolution and reports intervention rates, but as a training-progress diagnostic without intervals, clustering, or anti-gaming checks [Luo2024, Liu2023, Hoque2021b, VERIFIED]. The taxonomy standards (SAE J3016, ISO 8373, IEEE 1872.2) classify capability or role, not measured performance; IEEE 7001 is the only standard that specifies what an autonomous system must log for later audit, and it does so for transparency rather than for autonomy measurement [Winfield2021, VERIFIED].

## Taxonomies of autonomy level

### SAE J3016 (on-road driving automation)

SAE J3016_202104 defines six levels of driving automation (Level 0 to Level 5) and states that the levels are defined by the role of each of three actors in performing the dynamic driving task (DDT) and the DDT fallback ([SAE-J3016](https://saemobilus.sae.org/standards/j3016_202104-taxonomy-definitions-terms-related-driving-automation-systems-road-motor-vehicles), CLAIM: official scope page read; full text paywalled). SAE describes the levels as "descriptive and informative, rather than normative, and technical rather than legal" (same source, CLAIM; wording seen in a third-party reproduction of the document). The DDT fallback (clause 3.12) is the response by the user to perform the DDT or achieve a minimal risk condition after a DDT performance-relevant system failure or ODD exit, or the equivalent response by an ADS; the minimal risk condition (clause 3.16) is a stable, stopped condition reached after fallback when a trip cannot or should not be continued (CLAIM: wording seen only in third-party reproductions and in the California DMV's citation of J3016 APR2021). California's 2019 regulations incorporate J3016 (SEP2016) by reference and reproduce the DDT definition in regulatory text: "all of the real-time functions required to operate a vehicle in on-road traffic, excluding selection of final and intermediate destinations" ([DMV-REG-2019](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/uploads/2020/06/Adopted-Regulatory-Text-2019.pdf) §227.02(g), VERIFIED).

What J3016 does and does not do for measurement: it defines *who is responsible* for the DDT when a feature is engaged, and it defines the categories of event (request to intervene, fallback, minimal risk condition) that a measurement system would have to log. It defines no unit of measurement, no rate, and no interval. The level is a design property of the feature, not an observed quantity. Remote operation is handled in J3016 as a role (remote driver / remote assistance) but the standard does not prescribe how to measure how much remote input a fleet receives (CLAIM).

### ISO 8373:2021 and ISO/TC 299

ISO 8373:2021 defines a robot as a "programmed actuated mechanism with a degree of autonomy to perform locomotion, manipulation or positioning" (3.1) and autonomy as the "ability to perform intended tasks based on current state and sensing, without human intervention" (3.2), adding in a note that for a particular application the degree of autonomy "can be evaluated according to the quality of decision-making and independence from human", with IEC/TR 60601-4-1 cited as an example of degree-of-autonomy metrics for medical electrical equipment ([ISO-8373](https://www.iso.org/standard/75539.html), VERIFIED for 3.1/3.2, text read in the official sample excerpt). The same excerpt defines an *operator* as the "person designated to start, monitor and stop the intended operation" (3.11) and *collaboration* as "operation by purposely designed robots and person working within the same space" (3.13) (VERIFIED). A note to the mobile-robot entry states that "in addition to autonomous operation, a mobile robot can have means to be remotely controlled" (VERIFIED, excerpt). ISO/TC 299's scope is "standardization in the field of robotics, excluding toys and military applications" ([ISO-TC299](https://committee.iso.org/home/tc299), CLAIM).

ISO 8373 therefore supplies the vocabulary (autonomy is explicitly defined *by the absence of human intervention*) but delegates quantification to application standards. It is unit-free, and the one metric it points to (IEC/TR 60601-4-1) is a design-classification scale for medical devices, not a field measurement. Remote control is acknowledged as a capability, not distinguished as a measured state.

### IEEE 1872.2-2021 (Autonomous Robotics ontology)

IEEE Std 1872.2-2021 extends the CORA ontology of IEEE 1872-2015 with concepts, definitions and axioms for autonomous robotics, intended to describe design patterns and architectures of autonomous systems in a unified way ([IEEE-1872.2](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/1872.2/7094/), CLAIM: publisher scope and OpenAlex abstract; full text paywalled). The IEEE 1872 family defines autonomy as a *role* a robot plays in a task: an "automated robot" acts as an automaton following scripted plans; a "fully autonomous robot" solves the task "without human intervention while adapting to operational and environmental conditions"; a teleoperated robot "takes no initiative and relies on continuous or nearly continuous input from the human operator" from a location off the robot ([IEEE-RAS-TERMS](https://www.ieee-ras.org/images/Standards/meeting_june_2021/9-Robot-Term-Discussion.pdf), CLAIM: IEEE RAS committee presentation). The working group's own account of the standard's purpose is in [Gonçalves et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1109/MRA.2021.3095993) (CLAIM) and the pre-standard ontology in [Olszewska et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/ROMAN.2017.8172300) (CLAIM).

For measurement, the useful content is the role vocabulary: it distinguishes teleoperated (continuous remote input, no initiative), semi-autonomous and fully autonomous roles, which maps directly onto the control states Autonomy Meter must log. It specifies no observable, no unit, no interval.

### IEEE 7001-2021 (transparency of autonomous systems)

IEEE 7001-2021 sets out "measurable, testable levels of transparency, so that autonomous systems can be objectively assessed, and levels of compliance determined" ([IEEE-7001](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/7001/6929/), CLAIM: scope as reproduced by national standards bodies; PDF is in the IEEE GET program). The design rationale is given by the working group in [Winfield et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2021.665729) (VERIFIED): five stakeholder groups (users, general public and bystanders, safety certification agencies, incident/accident investigators, lawyers and expert witnesses), each with a 0 to 5 scale where every level is a qualitative, present-or-absent requirement. The incident-investigator ladder is the relevant one here. Level 2 requires "a data logging system capable of recording a date and time stamped record of robot sensor inputs, user commands, and actuator outputs"; Level 3 requires that the logging "conform to an existing open or industry standard, and additionally log high level decisions"; Level 4 adds logging "the reasons for the robot's high level decisions"; Level 5 adds visualisation tools for investigators (Winfield et al. 2021, VERIFIED). The authors motivate this with the aircraft flight data recorder and their earlier "ethical black box" proposal (VERIFIED).

This is the only standard found that mandates logging of *user commands* alongside actuator outputs with timestamps, exactly the raw material for distinguishing human from AI control. It does not, however, address log integrity (hash chains, signatures), define how to aggregate commands into an autonomy share, or require any statistical reporting.

### NIST ALFUS (Autonomy Levels for Unmanned Systems)

ALFUS, developed by a NIST-led federal working group from 2003, characterizes an unmanned system's Contextual Autonomous Capability (CAC) along three axes, mission complexity (MC), environmental complexity (EC) and human independence (HI), each with a set of metrics; the level of autonomy proper is the HI axis ([NIST-SP1011-II](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=823618), VERIFIED; [Huang2007](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=823619), VERIFIED; [Huang2005a](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=824538), VERIFIED). The HI metrics listed in Volume II are: UMS-to-operator communications / robotic initiation (ordinal); **"Ratio of Human Intervention Time / Mission Time"**, described as "the portion of the time during which operators are interacting with the UMS", on a "linear scale from approaching 0 % through 100 %", with the note that under mixed-initiative collaboration this "might be described and measured as neglect tolerance"; ratio of human planning time / mission time; level of human interaction (from "assign mission" down to "servo"); and level of human workload (NIST-SP1011-II §4, VERIFIED). The executive (summary) model maps these to a 0 to 10 scale whose HI column is expressed in interacting-time bands: levels 7 to 9 "interacting time between 6 % and 35 %", levels 4 to 6 "between 36 % and 65 %", levels 1 to 3 "between 66 % & 95 %", level 10 "approaching zero human interaction", level 0 "remote control" (NIST-SP1011-II Table 1, VERIFIED). ALFUS also distinguishes four operational modes, fully autonomous, semi-autonomous, teleoperation and remote control, and states that these modes are distinct from autonomy levels (VERIFIED). Volume I (terminology) exists as NIST SP 1011-I-2.0 ([NIST-SP1011-I](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.1011-I-2.0), CLAIM: landing page only; PDF host not reachable).

ALFUS is the closest prior art to a time-based autonomy share: it names the ratio, gives its range, and even bins it into levels. What it lacks is everything needed to make the number defensible: a definition of what counts as "interacting" (monitoring versus commanding versus approving), an estimator, an interval, treatment of unobserved time, any mention of remote versus co-located humans, and any defence against manipulation. ALFUS Volume II itself notes that the human-interaction-time metric "might be further decomposed to actuation time, monitoring time, sensory data acquisition time, etc." and that "further investigation of this issue is planned" (VERIFIED); this is a decomposition that, to our knowledge, was never standardized.

### Levels-of-automation scales from human factors

The HI axis of ALFUS and the role ladder of IEEE 1872 descend from the 10-level Sheridan–Verplank scale of 1978 ([Sheridan1978](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19790007441), UNKNOWN: NTRS not reachable; described in Beer et al. 2014, VERIFIED), which Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens generalized to four function classes (information acquisition, information analysis, decision and action selection, action implementation), each automatable along a continuum from manual to fully automatic ([Parasuraman2000](https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354), CLAIM: abstract). Beer, Fisk and Rogers adapt this into a 10-point Levels of Robot Autonomy (LORA) taxonomy for HRI and define autonomy, for machines, in terms of "performing aspects of a task without human intervention" ([Beer2014](https://doi.org/10.5898/JHRI.3.2.Beer), VERIFIED). These scales classify design intent (what the machine *may* do), and all three say so; none measures what fraction of a deployment's work the machine *did*.

### ASTM F45 (industrial driverless vehicles)

ASTM Committee F45 (originally "Driverless Automatic Guided Industrial Vehicles", now "Robotics, Automation, and Autonomous Systems") has published F3200 (terminology), F3218 (recording environmental effects), F3244 (navigation: defined area), F3265 (grid-video obstacle measurement) and F3327 (recording the A-UGV test configuration) ([ASTM-F45](https://www.astm.org/get-involved/technical-committees/committee-f45), CLAIM; [NIST-F45-page](https://www.nist.gov/el/intelligent-systems-division-73500/unmanned-ground-vehicles-research-and-standard-test-methods-4), CLAIM). F3200 defines an A-UGV as an "automatic, automated or autonomous vehicle that operates while in contact with the ground without a human operator" ([Bostelman-F3200](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=927496), CLAIM: quoted in a NIST paper). F45 therefore measures *task performance* of a driverless vehicle under controlled test conditions (can it navigate a defined area, dock, see an obstacle); the human is defined out of the test. The committee's own 2017 minutes record that "test methods specific to level of autonomy will be required" ([NIST F45 meeting notes, Dec 2017](https://www.nist.gov/document/mtg6minutes-dec2017pdf), CLAIM), which we read as acknowledgement that F45 does not yet measure autonomy level. No F45 document found addresses interventions during deployment.

### China T/CIE 298-2025 (humanoid robot intelligence grading)

T/CIE 298-2025, 《人形机器人智能化分级》 (Humanoid Robot Intelligence Grading), is a group standard of the China Institute of Electronics released in May 2025, drafted under the lead of the Beijing Humanoid Robot Innovation Center with the Shanghai and Zhejiang centers, UBTECH, Unitree, CAICT and others ([TCIE-298](https://www.x-humanoid.com/m/news-view-202.html), VERIFIED: lead drafter's announcement; [BeijingEtown-TCIE](https://www.beijingetown.com.cn/2025-05/28/c_1096597.htm), CLAIM). The announcement describes a "four-dimensional, five-level" framework: capability dimensions perception-cognition (P), decision-learning (D), execution-performance (E) and collaboration-interaction (C), graded L1–L5 with capability increasing by level; 22 primary indicators and over 100 technical clauses, a general safety baseline and mappings to typical application scenarios; and explicitly states that it borrows grading logic from autonomous driving and industrial robots (TCIE-298, VERIFIED for the announcement; the standard's clause text was not located, UNKNOWN). Like J3016 it is a capability classification: nothing in the announcement indicates a field-measured autonomy share, intervals, or logging requirements.

## Regulatory measurement: California DMV disengagement reporting

California is the only jurisdiction found that has for a decade required a *measured* human-intervention statistic from autonomous vehicle operators. Under 13 CCR §227.50 (2019 text), a "disengagement" is "a deactivation of the autonomous mode when a failure of the autonomous technology is detected or when the safe operation of the vehicle requires that the autonomous vehicle test driver disengage the autonomous mode and take immediate manual control of the vehicle, or in the case of driverless vehicles, when the safety of the vehicle, the occupants of the vehicle, or the public requires that the autonomous technology be deactivated" ([DMV-REG-2019](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/uploads/2020/06/Adopted-Regulatory-Text-2019.pdf) §227.50(a), VERIFIED). The annual report (December 1 to November 30) must give, per disengagement, location class, whether a driver was present, a plain-language description of the facts, and "the party that initiated the disengagement (autonomous technology, autonomous vehicle test driver, remote operator, or passenger)", plus "the total number of miles each autonomous vehicle tested in autonomous mode on public roads each month" (§227.50(b)(3)–(4), VERIFIED). Form OL 311R implements this with one row per event (date, VIN, initiated by, location, description, driverless-capable yes/no, driver present yes/no) and a per-VIN monthly mileage table ([DMV-OL311R](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/annual-report-of-autonomous-vehicle-disengagement-ol-311r-pdf/), VERIFIED). The regulation also defines "autonomous mode" as operation where technology "remote and/or on-board, performs the dynamic driving task, with or without a natural person actively supervising", and defines an autonomous test vehicle as one that "requires a human test driver or a remote operator to continuously supervise" (§227.02(a)–(b), VERIFIED). The resulting yearly CSV files (2019–20 through 2024, with separate driverless and first-time-filer files) are published by the DMV ([DMV-CSV-2024](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/2024-autonomous-vehicle-disengagement-reports-csv/), VERIFIED: located via the DMV search API).

Three properties of this regime matter for Autonomy Meter. First, the unit is an *event* (a disengagement) and the exposure is *distance* (autonomous miles); time in autonomous mode, time under manual control, and the duration of each takeover are not reported, so a time autonomy share cannot be computed from the data. Second, the definition is safety-scoped: a takeover that was not required for safety, a remote-assistance interaction that did not deactivate autonomous mode, or a stop instead of a takeover need not be reported; these are exactly the "calling help assist" and "stopping instead of taking over" gaming routes in the brief. Third, the DMV itself disclaims comparability: its February 2026 release states that the reports "are intended to provide insight into the operational activities of each permit holder and are not designed for comparative analysis across companies", that private-road, out-of-state, sub-Level-3 and simulation testing are excluded, and that "disengagement reporting is not required for AV operations conducted under deployment permits" ([DMV-NEWS-2026](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/news-and-media/autonomous-vehicle-permit-holders-in-california-logged-more-than-9-million-test-miles-between-december-1-2024-and-november-30-2025/), VERIFIED).

California is now replacing this regime. The Order to Adopt posted as the current Article 3.7 removes disengagement reporting and adds §227.56 "Reporting of Dynamic Driving Task Performance Relevant System Failures", which requires monthly CSV reports of "each instance in which an autonomous vehicle test driver took over performance of the dynamic driving task, or performed the dynamic driving task fallback, in response to a dynamic driving task performance relevant system failure", with date/time, coordinates to four decimals, and a cause category (software, hardware, other road users, weather, road surface/traffic, construction, loss of communication, other emergencies, collisions) ([DMV-REG-CURRENT](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/order-to-adopt-article-3-7-regulations-pdf/) §227.56, VERIFIED). A "dynamic driving task performance relevant system failure" is "a malfunction in an automated driving system and/or other vehicle system that prevents the automated driving system from reliably performing its portion of the dynamic driving task on a sustained basis" (§227.02, VERIFIED). §227.58 requires monthly reporting of "vehicle immobilizations", defined as a stop in an active travel lane when a driverless vehicle "is not able to continue the dynamic driving task and must be retrieved or requires the vehicle to be driven by a human driver at the scene or a remote driver"; §227.60 requires monthly autonomous-mode miles disaggregated by drivered and driverless permit (VERIFIED). The new text also defines a "remote driver" as a person not in the driver's seat who performs "real time performance of part or all of the dynamic driving task and/or the dynamic driving task fall back", and "remote operations support" as any function by a remotely located human including "remote assistance, remote driving, customer support, or dispatching" (§227.02, VERIFIED). The DMV's release confirms the intent: the new rules "replace disengagement reporting requirements" with failure, immobilization, hard-braking, mileage and crash reporting aligned to J3016 APR2021 and the NHTSA Standing General Order (DMV-NEWS-2026, VERIFIED). Reporting under §§227.56–227.60 becomes operative 120 days after the regulations' effective date (DMV-REG-CURRENT, VERIFIED; the effective date itself was not found in the order text, UNKNOWN).

Two consequences for Autonomy Meter follow. The regulator with the longest record of intervention counting has concluded that safety-scoped disengagement counts are not a comparable autonomy metric and has moved to a failure-scoped metric that is *narrower*, not broader: driver takeovers for reasons other than a system failure, and all remote-assistance interactions short of remote driving, fall outside §227.56. And California has now written the remote/local distinction into regulatory definitions (remote driver versus remote operations support versus on-scene human driver), which Autonomy Meter can adopt rather than invent.

### Peer-reviewed critiques of miles-per-disengagement

The research literature on the California data converges on the same limitations. Dixit, Chand and Nair computed disengagement exposure from the first reports and found it spanning three orders of magnitude, "from approximately 1.1 disengagements per 1000 miles for Google to 980 disengagements per 1000 miles for Mercedes-Benz", with Google falling to "around 0.18 disengagements per 1000 miles" by the end of the period (the 1.1 figure is the period average), split disengagements into automatic (system-detected failure) and manual (driver-initiated), and estimated a mean takeover reaction time of 0.83 s ([Dixit2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0168054), VERIFIED). Favarò, Eurich and Nader analysed 2014–2017 reports and, per their abstract, found that the analysis "highlights several shortcomings of the current regulations" in wording and structure ([Favaro2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aap.2017.11.001), CLAIM: abstract fragments; paywalled). Lv et al. classified first-year disengagements by attribute and cause and studied takeover mechanisms and times ([Lv2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/JAS.2017.7510745), CLAIM). Banerjee et al. report 1,116,605 autonomous miles, 5,328 disengagements and 42 accidents across 144 vehicles for 2014–2017 and attribute 64 % of disengagements to the machine-learning perception and decision-and-control stack ([Banerjee2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/DSN.2018.00066), CLAIM: abstract). Lee, Long and Choi (2025) use the DMV initiator field to separate human-initiated from technological/environmental disengagements and find human-related disengagements decreasing while technological and environmental ones increase, which they interpret as vehicles being pushed into harder scenarios ([Lee2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2025.e42747), CLAIM: abstract). On the statistical side, Kalra and Paddock show that demonstrating safety from exposure alone would require "hundreds of millions of miles and sometimes hundreds of billions of miles" ([Kalra2016](https://doi.org/10.7249/RR1478), CLAIM: abstract), the same rare-event arithmetic that governs any zero-intervention claim.

None of these papers reports a confidence interval on a per-company rate, models overdispersion across vehicles or months, or treats the miles-per-disengagement ratio as a censored time-to-event quantity; all treat the regulator's definition as given. The literature has described the measurement problem; it has not solved it.

## Shared autonomy and teleoperation: continuous, not binary, control

The shared-control literature shows that "who is in control" is often a continuous quantity rather than a switch. Dragan and Srinivasa formalize assistance as *policy blending*: the robot predicts user intent and an arbitration function combines the user's input with the robot's prediction, and they argue arbitration "should be contextual: it should depend on the robot's confidence in itself and in the user" ([Dragan2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364913490324), CLAIM: abstract). Javdani et al. cast shared autonomy as a POMDP solved by hindsight optimization ([Javdani2015](https://doi.org/10.15607/RSS.2015.XI.032), CLAIM) and, in the journal version, describe blending as treating "user actions and full autonomy ... as two independent sources, which are combined by some arbitration function that determines the relative contribution of each", noting that autonomous takeover, potential fields and virtual fixtures are all special cases ([Javdani2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364918776060), VERIFIED: arXiv full text). Losey et al. organize physical human–robot shared control into intent detection, arbitration and feedback ([Losey2018](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.4039145), CLAIM: abstract). Bilateral teleoperation has its own half-century of control theory ([Hokayem2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.automatica.2006.06.027), CLAIM: bibliographic record only), and humanoid teleoperation adds whole-body retargeting across many limbs, often with different limbs under different control sources ([Darvish2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2023.3236952), CLAIM: abstract).

For a measurement standard this literature establishes two requirements. A binary ai/human state is insufficient when an arbitration weight exists; the log must carry either the weight or a declared `shared` state with a stated attribution rule. And per-body-part attribution on humanoids is not exotic: it is how teleoperation systems are built.

## Human-in-the-loop robot learning: interventions as logged events

The interactive imitation learning literature is the only body of work that logs human interventions at timestep resolution and reports them as a rate. DAgger itself does not: it queries the expert for labels on learner-visited states and contains no notion of a human taking control ([Ross2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686), VERIFIED: full text, zero occurrences of "intervention"). HG-DAgger introduces the human as a *gating function*: "the novice policy is rolled out until the expert observes that the novice has entered an unsafe region of the state space. The expert takes control and guides the system back", labels are collected only while the expert has control, a doubt threshold is learned from "the novice's doubt at the point of human intervention", and performance is reported as collisions and road departures per meter ([Kelly2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2019.8793698), VERIFIED: arXiv). EIL argues that "any amount of expert feedback, whether by intervention or non-intervention, provides information" and learns collision avoidance from "about one minute" of expert control ([Spencer2020](https://doi.org/10.15607/RSS.2020.XVI.055), CLAIM: abstract). Wayve's "Learning to Drive in a Day" uses the simplest exposure metric of all: the reward is "the distance travelled by the vehicle without the safety driver taking control", and an episode ends when the driver intervenes ([Kendall2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.00412), VERIFIED).

The Berkeley and Stanford/UT-Austin lines make the human's cost explicit. IWR is built on remote teleoperation: operators "monitor policy execution and intervene when they would like to take over control using a web browser and a smartphone", and each trajectory is split into policy-controlled and human-controlled segments, with the intervention segments up-weighted by α = |D_R|/|D_I| ([Mandlekar2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.06733), VERIFIED). LazyDAgger defines the burden of an intervention as two context switches each costing a latency L in timesteps plus the time spent intervening, and reports 60 % fewer context switches than SafeDAgger ([Hoque2021a](https://doi.org/10.1109/CASE49439.2021.9551469), VERIFIED). ThriftyDAgger formalizes total supervisor burden as B(π) = C(π)·(L + I(π)), i.e. expected context switches times (latency plus expected supervisor actions per intervention), and allocates a fixed intervention budget by novelty and risk ([Hoque2021b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08273), VERIFIED). Sirius deploys a human–robot team whose task success is 100 % by construction and separately reports the *autonomous* policy's success rate and "human workload as the percentage of intervention in the trajectories in each round" ([Liu2023](https://doi.org/10.15607/RSS.2023.XIX.005), VERIFIED). HIL-SERL gives the most precise operational definition found: "we report the intervention rate, for which we calculate the ratio of intervened timesteps to total timesteps within an episode and report a running average over 20 episodes", and notes that for the HG-DAgger baseline "since interventions occur frequently, leading to successful outcomes, the true policy success rate is likely lower than the curve suggests" ([Luo2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21845), VERIFIED).

The brief asked whether RoboCat- or ALOHA-style papers report intervention rates. The full texts of RoboCat ([Bousmalis2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11706), VERIFIED) and ALOHA/ACT ([Zhao2023](https://doi.org/10.15607/RSS.2023.XIX.016), VERIFIED) contain no occurrence of "intervention"; both report success rates only. Generalist-policy evaluations in this style therefore do not supply intervention data, and the claim that they do should be treated as unsupported.

Taken together, this literature already contains the right primitives: a timestep-level control-state log with a human/robot flag, a time-based intervention rate (HIL-SERL, Sirius), a count-based rate (context switches), a cost model that separates switch latency from intervention duration (ThriftyDAgger), remote operators as the intervening party (IWR), and the observation that team success conceals policy failure (HIL-SERL). What it lacks is the measurement-science layer: no paper reports a confidence interval on an intervention rate, models clustering across episodes or operators, treats intervention-free episodes as censored, or considers that the party being measured might shape the log.
